h1: CBAM's planned 2028 extension to downstream goods: what Turkish makers of machinery, parts and appliances should prepare
---

On 18 November 2026 the Istanbul think tank EDAM (Centre for Economics and Foreign Policy Studies) published a policy report by Sinan Ülgen on the planned extension of the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) to steel- and aluminium-intensive downstream goods. EDAM estimates that the share of Türkiye's exports to the EU covered by CBAM could rise from 11.4% today to between 21% and 34%. On a full-implementation basis, it puts the extra cost to Turkish exporters at EUR 210-435 million a year. Which end of that range applies would depend on the product list the EU institutions finally agree, whether at the 30 November 2026 trilogue or later.

This piece is not for steel mills. It is for the Turkish maker of fasteners, wire, springs, machinery, auto parts, cables or household appliances who has heard that CBAM will reach finished goods from 2028 and is asking: am I in, and what data will my EU customer need from me?

## What is proposed: COM(2025) 989 and 1 January 2028

On 17 December 2025 the European Commission tabled COM(2025) 989, amending the CBAM Regulation (EU) 2023/956 to extend its scope to downstream goods and add anti-circumvention measures. The Commission says 180 products are added. 94% of them are industrial supply-chain goods used in heavy machinery and specialised equipment, with an average steel and aluminium content of 79% by weight; the remaining 6% are household goods such as washing machines. Under Article 2 of the proposal, the extension applies from 1 January 2028.

The proposal also counts pre-consumer steel and aluminium scrap as a precursor and lets the Commission demand extra evidence where it suspects abusive practices. The file is still in the ordinary legislative procedure and EUR-Lex lists it as "ongoing". Until a text is adopted and published in the Official Journal, today's scope stands.

Screws, bolts and nuts (CN 7318) and "other articles of iron or steel" (7326) are already in CBAM today. The proposal adds items such as stranded wire, ropes and cables (7312 10), nails (7317 00), certain springs (7320), certain household articles (7323) and other cast articles (7325), plus a new "Combined metal products" table covering washing machines (8450), certain goods vehicles (ex 8704), gearboxes (8708 40), road wheels (8708 70), suspension systems (8708 80) and metal office furniture (9403 10).

## Three positions side by side

The institutions do not disagree on the 2028 start. They disagree on the length of the list.

- **Commission (17 December 2025):** 180 downstream products.
- **Council (general approach, 12 June 2026, ST 10423/26):** keeps the Commission list, adds about 200 eight-digit CN codes and removes three medical-device entries. The largest groups are vehicle parts (8708), electric motors (8501), refrigerators and freezers (8418) and transformers (8504). The Council also asks the Commission for an annual review of further downstream goods.
- **Parliament (15 September 2026, P10_TA(2026)0276):** amendments adopted by 464 votes to 50 with 159 abstentions, and the file referred back to committee for trilogue. Parliament adds the whole of Chapter 73 (articles of iron or steel), about two dozen other full headings and more than 270 eight-digit codes, so there is no single count: Mayer Brown says 457 product types, EDAM 623 CN lines on its widest reading. Parliament also proposes a separate 5-tonne de minimis threshold for aluminium products.

## Under the proposal, only the CBAM inputs would count

The key rule is the calculation boundary. Recital 42 of the proposal limits the emissions attributed to downstream goods to those embedded in the input materials (precursors). Recital 45 says the specific embedded emissions of goods in the "Iron and steel", "Aluminium" and "Combined metal goods" sections are calculated from the precursors the good contains.

The Commission and Türkiye's Ministry of Trade use the same example: a car door made outside the EU would face CBAM only for the emissions embedded in the steel sheet used to make it, not for cutting, forming or assembly. So the emissions of your press shop, welding line or paint line would not be counted. What would count is the quantity of each CBAM input in your product, mostly steel and aluminium, and the embedded emissions of that input. Recital 42 and the new Annex VIII add that inputs outside today's Annex II, such as pre-consumer scrap, are also counted.

For complex goods built from inputs of several sectors (the Commission's example is the washing machine), actual data will be hard to collect, so the proposal would apply default values without a mark-up. It also adds "material composition of each downstream good" to the minimum content of a verification report, and makes registration of the installation in the CBAM Registry a condition for declaring actual emissions.

## Still open before the 30 November trilogue

**Status (19 November 2026):** no agreement announced.

No EU institution has published a readout of the first political trilogue on 20 October 2026. According to Agence Europe, the two sides presented their mandates and agreed to continue technical work on four issues (positions from the institutions' texts):

1. **Aluminium de minimis:** today an importer whose CBAM imports stay below 50 tonnes net mass a year is exempt. Parliament wants a 5-tonne threshold for aluminium products; the Commission and Council keep the general 50-tonne threshold.
2. **Scrap:** the Commission and Council would count pre-consumer steel and aluminium scrap as a precursor. Parliament would count all aluminium scrap, including post-consumer, drop steel scrap from the precursor list and require scrap content to be reported.
3. **Online platforms:** Parliament wants reporting obligations for platforms acting as importers in distance sales.
4. **Global default values for the added goods.**

cbam.news reported on 18 November that 30 November is the second and possibly final political trilogue. Even after a political deal, the text only takes effect once Parliament and Council formally adopt it and it is published in the Official Journal.

## Why Türkiye is more exposed

Citing the Commission's impact assessment, the Ministry of Trade says Türkiye is the second most affected country after China, with Turkish exports of about EUR 8 billion a year brought into scope under the Commission's preferred option. EDAM's figure for the same list, below, is EUR 9.4 billion; data and methods differ.

EDAM's 18 November report breaks this down by list (2022–2024 averages, Eurostat data):

- Of Türkiye's average EUR 97.6 billion of annual exports to the EU, EUR 11.2 billion (11.4%) is already covered by CBAM.
- Newly covered exports would be EUR 9.4 billion under the Commission list, EUR 15.7 billion under the Council list and EUR 21.6 billion under Parliament's. The total covered share would rise to 21.1%, 27.6% and 33.5% respectively.
- The estimated extra cost, assuming full implementation, is EUR 210, 332 and 435 million a year.
- Vehicle parts, trucks, refrigerators, washing machines and insulated cables make up about 45% of the estimated cost under the Parliament list. Passenger cars, Türkiye's largest export to the EU, appear in none of the three lists.
- Türkiye supplies 81% of the EU's non-EU imports of steel radiators, 72% of semi-trailers and 55% of household dishwashers.

EDAM finds that most of Türkiye's newly covered exports are goods where the carbon cost exceeds 1% of the product price. It therefore concludes that a negotiating strategy aimed at shortening the list would offer Türkiye only limited protection. Its priorities are instead the deduction of carbon prices paid in Türkiye (Article 9) and completing installation-level monitoring, reporting and verification before 2028.

These are EDAM's estimates, not installation data. The report assumes full implementation, does not deduct any price paid under the Turkish ETS, counts partly covered "ex" lines in full, which it says overstates coverage, and itself notes that the gradual phase-out of EU ETS free allocation will significantly reduce the actual burden in the short and medium term.

## CN code check: a three-step self-screen

1. **Getting the code.** The starting point is the eight-digit CN code on the EU customer's import declaration. If it is already in today's Annex I (for example 7318 screws and bolts), this is a 2026 issue, not a 2028 one; the first read is our guide to [default values versus actual emissions](/en/insights/cbam-default-values-2026-turkish-steel-aluminium).
2. **Comparing all three texts.** The code is then checked against Annex I of the Commission proposal, Note 1 of the Council general approach and the text adopted by Parliament. Entries marked "ex" cover only part of a code, usually goods containing steel or aluminium. A code in all three texts is the most likely to stay; a code only in the Council or Parliament list depends on the final deal, on 30 November or later. Our [EU rule scope scanner](/en/decision-lab/eu-rule-scope-scanner) is a quick first pass.
3. **Recording the steel and aluminium content.** This step records the steel and aluminium mass per product and the plant that supplied the metal. The [CBAM readiness check](/en/decision-lab/cbam-readiness-check) helps you gauge where you stand.

## Why supplier emissions data matters now

Because the calculation rests on precursors, most of the data will come from your steel and aluminium suppliers rather than from you. Information to request from suppliers:

- The name, country and CBAM Registry registration of the installation that produced the metal.
- The CN code of the precursor delivered and the quantity per batch.
- The specific embedded emissions of that precursor, and whether an accredited verifier has verified them.
- The scrap content, and whether it is pre-consumer or post-consumer.
- Evidence of any carbon price paid.

On the manufacturer's side, a bill of materials with the mass of each steel and aluminium part matters, because the proposal would put material composition into the verification report. We cover choosing a verifier in our [CBAM verifier guide](/en/insights/cbam-verifier-turkish-installations-2026) and the logic of asking steel suppliers for data in our piece on the [steel digital product passport](/en/insights/steel-digital-product-passport-espr-turkiye).

EDAM flags one more point: for downstream goods, any carbon price deduction will rest on the price paid by the steel or aluminium supplier, not by the manufacturer of the finished good, and how that passes down the chain into the declaration is not yet clear. Writing into supply contracts now who provides this data, in what format and by when will make the 2028 price conversation with your buyer easier. ## FAQ

:::faq
### Is the product list final?
No. As of 19 November 2026 trilogue negotiations are ongoing, with the next political round on 30 November. The list is only final once the adopted text is published in the Official Journal.
### Will I pay CBAM?
Not directly. The authorised CBAM declarant in the EU, usually the importer, buys and surrenders the certificates (Regulation (EU) 2023/956, Article 22). Your role is to supply the data behind the declaration. The cost is still likely to come up in price negotiations.
### Will default values apply to my product?
They may. Without actual, verified data, default values apply. The Commission proposes no mark-up for complex goods with inputs from several sectors, while global default values for the added goods are one of the open issues in the negotiations.
### Does the 50-tonne threshold protect small exporters?
The threshold looks at the EU importer's total annual imports, not at you. The Commission says more than 90% of importers in the extension sectors would stay exempt while over 99% of emissions remain covered. Parliament wants a 5-tonne threshold for aluminium products.
### Does anything change in 2026 or 2027?
Under the proposal the downstream extension applies from 1 January 2028. If your product is already in today's scope (screws and bolts, for example), obligations have applied since 2026. Some goods entering the EU in 2028 will be produced in 2027, so in our view it makes sense to set up the data flow during 2027.
### Are passenger cars covered?
According to EDAM's analysis, passenger cars (8703) are in none of the three lists. Certain trucks and vehicle parts are in all three; semi-trailers appear in the Council and Parliament texts.
:::
